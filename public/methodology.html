How the prices are calculated

The short version: medians of real completed eBay sales, over a rolling 45-day window, and nothing at all when there is too little data to be honest about.

Where the sales come from

We maintain our own dataset of completed eBay sales for football cards. Every listing is parsed into its parts — year, set, player, card number, parallel, grader and grade — so that sales of the same card can be grouped together even when sellers describe them differently.

Only completed sales count. An asking price is a hope; a completed sale is evidence.

Median, not average

A single set runs from a two-dollar base card to a one-of-one patch autograph that sells for hundreds. The mean of that range is a number no card in the set has ever sold for. The median — the middle sale — describes what a typical card actually trades at, which is the question a collector is asking.

The same logic applies within a card. A card's figure is the middle of its own completed sales, so one unusually high or low sale moves it very little.

A set's headline figure is a median across its cards, not across its sales. Otherwise one heavily-traded base card, selling hundreds of times, would decide the number for the entire set.

The 45-day window

Prices are drawn from the last 45 days of sales, and the exact date range is printed on every price block. A price with no date attached to it is a claim about the present that quietly goes stale; showing the window means you can judge for yourself how current the figure is.

It also means a set that was hot six months ago and is quiet now shows no recent figure rather than an old one dressed up as current.

Conditions and grades are mixed — and we say so

The set and player page medians include raw cards and graded cards together. A PSA 10 and a raw copy of the same card are genuinely different markets, so a combined median sits somewhere between them and is not the value of either one.

That is why every price block says so in plain words. For a grade-by-grade breakdown of a specific card, search for it directly — the search results separate raw, PSA 10, PSA 9 and the rest.

When we show nothing

A page only shows prices if it has at least 20 completed sales across at least 10 different cards in the window. Below that, the median is noise.

Three sales of a common base card tell you nothing reliable about the fourth, and a page printing “median $4.00” from three sales looks authoritative while being close to meaningless. We would rather a page stay quiet than mislead, so pages under that bar show no price block at all.

What we do not do

  • No estimated values. If a card has not sold recently, we do not invent a figure for it by extrapolating from similar cards.
  • No asking prices presented as values. Current listings are shown as listings, clearly separate from completed sales.
  • No paid placement. No set, card or seller is featured in exchange for money, and affiliate links never alter a displayed price.

Known limitations

Worth stating plainly, because every price tool has these and few list them:

  • Title parsing is imperfect. Prices are built from seller-written listing titles. Most parse cleanly; some do not, and a mis-read title can put a sale under the wrong card. Volume limits the damage — one bad row barely moves a median — but it is not zero.
  • Shared card numbering. Inserts often reuse the base set's numbering, so some cards cannot be attributed to one subset with certainty. Where that is ambiguous, we leave the card out of the subset figure rather than guess at it.
  • Two people, one name. Fathers and sons share names in football. Where a sale could belong to either, it is counted for neither rather than assigned to the wrong player.
  • Photos come from the listings. eBay removes images from long-ended listings, so some older sales show a placeholder instead of a picture.
  • Recent sales only. The window is 45 days. This is not a long-run price history, and it is not trying to be one.
